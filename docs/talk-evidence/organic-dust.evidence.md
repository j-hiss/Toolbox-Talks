# Evidence: organic-dust

Fetched 2026-10-08 via WebFetch: NIOSH 94-102 landing page (https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/94-102) and full PDF (https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/158175/cdc_158175_DS1.pdf); OSHA Youth in Agriculture eTool, Organic Dust (https://www.osha.gov/etools/youth-agriculture/organic-dust); OSHA 1928.21.
Note: the fetch tool returned close summaries rather than full verbatim text for the eTool page; lines marked "paraphrase" match those summaries.

## No OSHA standard (important)
- 1910.134 (respiratory protection) is in Part 1910 Subpart I. 1928.21(b): except as listed in (a), Part 1910 subparts B through T and Z "do not apply to agricultural operations." 1910.134 is not in the (a) list. So no OSHA respirator standard applies to farm dust work; this talk uses NIOSH and OSHA guidance only (kind "guidance") and avoids "must" rules on fit testing/medical evaluation.
- Reviewer note: the existing library talk `respirators` (code 1910.134 / 1926.103) lists industry "ag". Its rule statements ("your company has to provide...", fit test annually, medical evaluation) do not apply to agricultural operations under 1928.21(b). Flag for the library owner. (EPA WPS / pesticide label respirator rules are separate and not covered here.)

## Hook
- "Dust from moldy hay, grain and animal barns can make you sick within hours." NIOSH 94-102 (paraphrase): fever typically 4 to 12 hours after exposure; sources include moldy silage, stored grain; eTool lists hay, grain, livestock.
- "It feels like the flu, so it's easy to brush off." NIOSH 94-102 (paraphrase): flu-like symptoms; often misdiagnosed as influenza.

## Where it comes from
- "Organic dust comes from hay, grain, straw and livestock. It carries molds, bacteria, pollen, bits of feed and bedding, and animal hair, feathers and droppings." eTool (paraphrase): dust from "hay, grain, straw, fuel chips, and livestock" including "molds, pollens, bacteria, pesticides, feed and bedding particles, and animal hair, feathers, and droppings." (Pesticides and fuel chips omitted for brevity.)
- "It gets heaviest in closed-in spaces like grain dryers, livestock pens and swine buildings." eTool (paraphrase): heavy concentrations common in grain dryers, livestock pens, swine buildings, other enclosed spaces.
- "The dustiest jobs include handling moldy hay, uncapping silos, cleaning grain bins, and working in dusty fields and buildings." eTool (paraphrase): tasks calling for respiratory protection: dusty fields and buildings, handling moldy hay, uncapping silos, cleaning grain bins.

## Know the signs
- "ODTS usually starts with a fever 4 to 12 hours after heavy dust. Weakness, headache, chills, body aches and a cough come with it. Some people get short of breath." NIOSH 94-102 (paraphrase): fever typically 4-12 hours after exposure, flu-like symptoms: weakness, headache, chills, body aches, cough; shortness of breath may occur.
- "It usually clears up within a day to a few days once you're away from the dust." NIOSH 94-102 (exact): "ODTS usually disappears within 24 hours to a few days after the worker is removed from exposure."
  - Reviewer note: the OSHA eTool says symptoms "can appear hours after exposure and last up to a week." Talk uses the NIOSH wording.
- "It often gets mistaken for the flu or bronchitis." NIOSH 94-102 (paraphrase): often misdiagnosed as bronchitis, influenza, or farmer's lung.
- "If you get sick after dusty work, get medical help early and tell the doctor about the dust." eTool (paraphrase): seek medical help early and tell your doctor about recent dust exposure. NIOSH: tell your doctor about recent dust exposure.
- "Breathing this dust over time can cause congestion, coughing or wheezing, and frequent infections. It's also tied to farmer's lung." eTool (paraphrase): long-term exposure can cause congestion, coughing or wheezing, dust sensitivity, frequent infections; can lead to ODTS and Farmer's Lung.

## Cut the dust first
- "NIOSH says a respirator should not be the only thing protecting you. Cut the dust at the source first." NIOSH 94-102 (exact): "Respirators should not be used as the only means of preventing or minimizing exposures to airborne contaminants." Plus engineering controls (local exhaust ventilation, wet suppression).
- "Wet down feed and silo tops before you uncap or move them. Wet the material when you clean out bins." eTool (paraphrase): wet down feed and silo tops before uncapping or transferring; wet materials when cleaning bins.
- "Keep the ventilation working. Use machines, not hands and shovels, to move moldy or rotting material when you can." eTool (paraphrase): maintain ventilation; use automated equipment for moldy materials. NIOSH: use automated or mechanized equipment to move decayed materials. ("hands and shovels" — NIOSH case reports involve shoveling oats and wood chips.)
- "Keeping hay, silage and grain dry, and drying grain before you store it, keeps mold down." eTool (paraphrase): control moisture in hay, silage and grain; dry grain before storage; also use mold inhibitors.

## When you need a respirator
- "A basic dust mask helps with nuisance dust. It does not protect you from organic vapors." eTool (paraphrase): a dust mask helps with nuisance dust but does not protect against organic vapors.
- "For grain or silage work in closed-in areas, wear the respirator your company gives you, like a NIOSH-approved N95." eTool (paraphrase): wear provided respirators, such as a NIOSH-approved N95, especially when handling grain or silage in enclosed areas. Employer responsibilities (guidance): provide NIOSH-certified respirators matched to the job.
- "A respirator only works if it fits. Ask for training on how to wear it and care for it, including a fit test." eTool (paraphrase): get training on respirator use and care, including fit testing; employers have respirators fit-tested. NIOSH 94-102: respiratory protection program should include fit testing, training. Stated as advice, not a legal rule (see 1928.21 note).
- "Take dusty work clothes off outside, before you go into the house." NIOSH 94-102 (paraphrase): don't wear contaminated work clothes inside the home; remove them outside.

Scope: there is no OSHA H2S-specific standard beyond the air contaminant limits (Z-2 general industry; 1926.55 construction, value read on ST). Practical lines are guidance.

|---|---|---|
| At high levels, hydrogen sulfide can drop you in one or two breaths. | guidance: HZ; FF | exact HZ (700-1000 ppm): "Rapid unconsciousness, "knockdown" or immediate collapse within 1 to 2 breaths"; FF: "concentrations exceeding 700 ppm can cause immediate collapse, and death, within just one or two breaths." |
| And the rotten-egg smell you count on to warn you can disappear. | guidance: OV; EC | exact OV: "you can no longer smell it to warn you it's there."; EC: "the ability to smell the gas can be lost instantly (olfactory paralysis)." |
| H2S is a colorless gas that smells like rotten eggs at low levels. | guidance: OV | exact: "a colorless gas known for its pungent 'rotten egg' odor at low concentrations." |
| It's highly flammable, and it's toxic even at low levels. | guidance: OV | exact: "It is highly flammable and toxic, even at low concentrations." |
| It's heavier than air. It can travel along the ground and collect in low spots and enclosed spaces like pits, manholes, tunnels and wells. | guidance: OV; ET; WP | exact OV: "It is heavier than air and may travel along the ground."; ET: "may travel along the ground or accumulate in low-lying areas."; WP: "Confined spaces (for example pits, manholes, tunnels, wells) where hydrogen sulfide can build up to dangerous levels." |
| You can find it in oil and gas wells, sewers, septic tanks and wastewater treatment. | guidance: OV; WP | exact OV: "occurs naturally in sewers, manure pits, well water, oil and gas wells"; WP: "cleaning or maintaining municipal sewers and septic tanks."; "Sewer and wastewater treatment" |
| After a while at low levels, or quickly at high levels, you can't smell it anymore. | guidance: OV | exact: "After a while at low or more quickly at high concentrations, you can no longer smell it to warn you it's there." |
| Never rely on your sense of smell to tell you H2S is there or how much is there. | guidance: EC; ET; QC | exact EC: "DO NOT rely on your sense of smell to indicate the continuing presence of hydrogen sulfide or to warn of harmful levels."; ET: "Never rely on sense of smell for the presence of H2S." |
| At 100 ppm and above, it's immediately dangerous to life and health. | guidance: EC; QC; FF | exact EC: "Exposures at or above 100 ppm are considered immediately dangerous to life and health (IDLH)." |
| At 700 ppm and up, people collapse within one or two breaths. | guidance: HZ; ET; FF | exact ET: "knockdown is almost immediate when H2S is at or above 700 ppm."; FF as above |
| OSHA's general industry limit is a 20 ppm ceiling, with one short exception for a peak up to 50 ppm. | 1910.1000 Table Z-2; guidance: ST | exact Z2: ceiling "20 ppm"; peak "50 ppm"; "10 mins. once, only if no other meas. exp. occurs." ST: "if no other measurable exposure occurs during the 8-hour work shift, exposures may exceed 20 ppm, but not more than 50 ppm (peak), for a single time period up to 10 minutes." |
| In construction the limit is 10 ppm averaged over the shift. | guidance: ST (29 CFR 1926.55) | exact: "Sets exposure limit of 10 ppm (15 mg/m3) time-weighted average (TWA)". | Wear your personal monitor as close to your breathing zone as you can, like on your collar or above your chest. | guidance: ET | exact: "Wearing personal monitors as close as possible to the breathing zone to be most effective (e.g., above the chest or on the collar)." |
| Monitors have to be maintained and calibrated. | guidance: ET | exact: "Ensure that monitors are properly maintained and calibrated." ("have to" reflects guidance wording "Ensure") |
| A detector left in test mode can't sound an alarm. That was part of a deadly release OSHA investigated. | guidance: FF | exact: "Some detectors had been set to a testing mode, preventing any alarm signals from being sent"; "A worker died of acute hydrogen sulfide (H2S) poisoning..." |
| Industry practice is usually an alarm at 10 ppm, and that alarm means get out. | guidance: ET | exact: "Generally, industry practice uses 10 ppm as an alarm set point to warn workers of the hazard, and to evacuate the area," |
| Know the wind direction from the windsock, your escape routes, and where the safe briefing area is. | guidance: ET (training topics) | exact: "Wind direction awareness (windsocks) and routes of egress."; "Locations of safe briefing areas." |
| H2S can almost instantly overcome unprotected workers, including rescuers. | guidance: ET; OV | exact ET: "can quickly – almost immediately – overcome unprotected workers, including rescue workers." |
| Don't go in after someone unless you're trained and wearing a supplied-air respirator like an SCBA. | guidance: ET; EC | exact ET: "Ensure the use of air-supplied respirators, such as self-contained breathing apparatus, for emergency responders who will be entering areas of unknown concentrations of H2S."; EC: "First responders must be trained and properly protected before entering areas with elevated levels of hydrogen sulfide." | Responders have to be trained and protected before they enter. | guidance: EC | exact: "First responders must be trained and properly protected before entering areas with elevated levels of hydrogen sulfide." |
